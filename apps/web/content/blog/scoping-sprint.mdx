We tell every new client the same thing: the first two weeks are a scoping sprint, and nothing gets built during them. We get questions about this. Sometimes push-back.

Here's what the two weeks are actually for.

## We're not evaluating feasibility

The question "can we build this?" is almost always yes. The thing we're evaluating is whether we should — and whether what you've described is actually the problem.

Most of the time, the thing a client asks for in the first meeting is not quite the thing they need. Not because they don't know their operations. Because they're used to describing pain in terms of the tasks they do, not the outcomes they need.

"We need an agent to summarise our carrier emails" is a task description. "Three of my coordinators spend four hours a day reading emails and deciding what to do with them" is the problem. The agent that solves the second problem is different from the agent that solves the first — and usually simpler.

## Day one to three: read the actual work

We ask to shadow the team. Not an interview. Not a process diagram. We sit with whoever does the work every day and read what they read.

For logistics clients, that means reading a hundred carrier emails. For finance clients, it means watching someone process invoices for an afternoon. For support teams, it means going through a week of tickets.

This is where scope dies or lives. If the emails are 80% structured and 20% weird edge cases, the agent can handle the 80% and escalate the 20%. If the emails are 80% weird edge cases, no model is going to outperform a sharp coordinator — and we'll tell you that.

## Day four to seven: find the seam

Every workflow has a seam — the point where human judgment adds genuine value and where it doesn't. The agent belongs on one side of that seam. The human belongs on the other.

Most of our time in week one is finding that seam precisely. Not approximately. The difference between "route everything with over 70% confidence" and "route everything with over 80% confidence" is not engineering — it's understanding what a wrong route costs versus what an unnecessary escalation costs.

We write this down. In plain English, not pseudocode. We show it to the people who do the work and ask if we got it right.

## Day eight to ten: the math

The scoping sprint always ends with a return calculation.

We estimate:
- How many tasks per week the agent will handle
- What percentage it will get right without escalation
- What it costs to process a misclassification
- What it costs per month to run the agent (compute + our operate fee)
- How many weeks before the savings exceed the build cost

If the math doesn't work out to under ten weeks, we tell you. We've walked away from projects where the timeline was longer. Not because we couldn't build it — because we couldn't justify it with a straight face.

## Day eleven to fourteen: the brief

The deliverable from the scoping sprint is a brief. Not a spec, not a roadmap — a brief. One page. It says:

- What problem we're solving
- Where the agent lives in the workflow
- What it handles, what it escalates
- What "done" looks like in measurable terms
- What success looks like in month three

We review it together before we start building. If something's wrong, we fix it now, not in week five.

---

The reason clients push back on the two-week scope is that it feels like delay. It isn't. It's the work. Everything that happens in the build and the operate phases goes better when we've spent two weeks not building.
