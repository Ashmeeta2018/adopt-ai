When we say "we stay on the pager for 90 days," clients sometimes picture a NOC with blinking dashboards. It's quieter than that. Here's what it actually looks like.

## What we're watching

Every agent produces three numbers we care about:

**Task volume.** How many items is it processing per hour? A drop in volume can mean the trigger stopped working — the webhook stopped firing, the email inbox isn't being polled, the schedule job failed.

**Escalation rate.** What percentage of items is the agent sending to a human? We expect this to drift over time as input data changes. A sudden jump means something upstream changed. A slow creep means the model is starting to see data it wasn't trained on.

**Error rate.** What percentage of items failed outright — not escalated, not routed, just failed? This is usually a downstream integration problem: the TMS API rate-limiting us, the QuickBooks OAuth token expiring, a schema change in the data we're writing.

We look at these three numbers twice a day during the first four weeks, once a day in weeks five through twelve, and weekly after that.

## The alert that wakes someone up

We only wake up a human for two things:

1. **Volume drops to zero for more than fifteen minutes during business hours.** This is always a broken trigger, not a model problem. The on-call person restarts the poller and files a post-mortem.

2. **Error rate exceeds 5% for more than ten minutes.** This is almost always a downstream integration issue. We don't touch the model. We look at the logs, find the failing integration, and fix it.

Escalation rate changes never wake us up. They're signals to review, not emergencies. We review them in the twice-weekly sync with the client.

## The on-call rotation

For a single-agent engagement, we have two people on rotation: the engineer who built the agent and one other. Two-week on-call cycles, overlapping by two days.

We use PagerDuty for the alerts. The integration is simple: the agent's monitoring script posts to a PagerDuty Events API endpoint when a threshold is breached. PagerDuty handles the escalation to the phone.

The on-call phone is a shared number with a SIM that we hand off physically between rotation shifts. Yes, physically. It's more reliable than virtual forwarding for 3 AM pages.

## The weekly sync

Every week, we send a written report to the client's ops lead. It covers:

- Volume for the week (compared to the previous week and to forecast)
- Escalation rate and whether it's trending
- Any incidents — what happened, how long it took to fix, whether it will happen again
- One recommendation for the following week

The report is one page. It takes us about thirty minutes to write. Most weeks, there's nothing dramatic in it.

## What the 90 days actually produces

By the end of the operate period, three things have happened.

**The model has been tuned at least once.** In 90 days of production data, we almost always find a cluster of misclassifications we can fix with additional training. We do one retrain, not many. We flag it to the client before we do it.

**The runbook exists.** By week twelve, we've documented every incident, every alert condition, and every common fix. The runbook is plain English, not pseudocode. The ops lead can follow it without us.

**Trust is established.** Clients don't hand over a critical workflow to an algorithm they've known for six weeks. They hand it over to a system that has run quietly for three months and has a track record. The 90-day operate period isn't about keeping the agent alive — it's about building the trust that lets us hand it off.
