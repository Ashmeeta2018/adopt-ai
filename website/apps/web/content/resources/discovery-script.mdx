This is the script we run in every two-hour scoping session. It's not a survey. It's a conversation guide — the questions are a skeleton, and the real work is listening for what's underneath the answer.

We've run this session with logistics coordinators, finops teams, healthcare practice managers, and e-commerce operators. The questions are the same every time. What changes is what the answers reveal.

## How to use this

Don't read the questions off a page. Know them well enough to ask them naturally. The most important part of each question is the follow-up: "can you give me a specific example from last week?"

Specificity is everything. "We have a lot of email" is not a scope. "We get 300 carrier emails a day, I personally read about 80 of them, and I'd say 20 require me to actually do something" is a scope.

## The seven questions

### 1. Walk me through what happens from the moment [the trigger] arrives.

Replace `[the trigger]` with whatever they described in the first conversation: an email, a document, a form submission, a customer message.

You're mapping the actual steps, not the intended steps. Listen for: handoffs between people, tools used at each step, decisions made and how they're made, and where things go wrong.

### 2. How much of that do you think is genuinely judgment-dependent?

You're trying to separate the classification / extraction task (automatable) from the escalation / decision task (human). Most people overestimate how much of their work requires real judgment.

Listen for: "well, most of the time it's obvious, but sometimes…" — that "sometimes" is where you're building.

### 3. What does a bad output look like, and what does it cost?

This is the most important question. The cost of a wrong action determines everything about the confidence gate, the escalation design, and whether the project is worth building.

Listen for: financial costs (a missed pickup, a wrong invoice line item), time costs (how long to unwind a mistake), and relationship costs (a carrier or customer affected by an error).

### 4. Who would review the agent's work before it's fully trusted?

Every agent needs a review phase. You're learning who has the judgment to catch errors and the time to do review work consistently.

Listen for: is this person available? Do they care about accuracy? Will they give you feedback on errors, or will they just fix them silently?

### 5. What does the data look like?

You need to understand the input data before you can assess whether a model will perform well on it. Ask to see real examples: actual emails, actual invoices, actual tickets.

Listen for: how structured the data is, how variable the formats are, whether there are recurring edge cases, and whether there are any obvious privacy constraints.

### 6. What tools does the output need to touch?

Integration complexity is often the hidden cost in a project. A well-classified email that goes nowhere is worthless. You need to understand where the agent's output needs to land.

Listen for: named tools (specific TMS, QuickBooks version, Zendesk plan), API access availability, IT constraints, and whether there's a staging environment for testing.

### 7. If this agent was running well six months from now, what would you most want to be different?

This is the outcome question. The answer tells you what the client actually cares about — which is often not the same as what they asked for.

Listen for: "I'd have my Tuesdays back," "my team would stop dreading Monday morning," "we'd finally be able to take on more volume without hiring." Those phrases are the real brief.

## After the session

Within 24 hours, write a one-page brief that covers:

1. The workflow being automated (one sentence)
2. The seam: what the agent handles vs. what it escalates
3. The return calculation: tasks/week, confidence threshold estimate, hours saved, payback in weeks
4. The integration chain: trigger → agent → output systems
5. The review owner and their availability
6. Open questions and blockers

Share it with the client before building anything. If they correct something, the brief saved you weeks of building the wrong thing.
